## What Is Compounding Pharmacy?
Compounding is the preparation of customised medications by licensed pharmacists to meet needs that commercially manufactured drugs do not, including adjusting dosage strengths, removing allergens or excipients, changing a medication's form (for example from tablet to liquid), and producing preparations that are otherwise commercially unavailable, particularly during drug shortages.

## 503A vs 503B Facilities

### 503A — Traditional Compounding
- Prepares patient-specific prescriptions.
- Regulated primarily at the state board level.
- Smaller-scale operations.
- Not required to register with the FDA as a manufacturer.

### 503B — Outsourcing Facilities
- Performs large-scale sterile compounding, and may compound without a patient-specific prescription.
- Registers with the FDA and is regulated much like a drug manufacturer, including current Good Manufacturing Practice expectations and routine inspection.
- **This is Sapho Bio's primary market**, together with precision-medicine and cell- and gene-therapy manufacturers who face the same sterility-release pressures.

## Key Regulations & Standards
- **USP <797> — Sterile Compounding:** environmental controls (ISO 5/7/8 cleanrooms), beyond-use dating, and personnel competency for sterile preparations.
- **USP <800> — Hazardous Drugs:** containment and personnel-protection requirements for handling hazardous drugs.
- **USP <71> — Sterility Tests:** the traditional compendial sterility test, requiring a fourteen-day incubation.
- **USP <1223> — Validation of Alternative Microbiological Methods:** the standard against which a rapid method such as Sapho Bio's is validated for equivalence to the compendial test. *(This is the standard Sapho Bio's platform has been validated against.)*
- **FDA cGMP for 503B:** current Good Manufacturing Practice, adverse-event reporting, product registration, and regular inspection.

## Quality & Compliance Challenges
Common operational pain points include environmental monitoring of cleanroom conditions, batch sterility testing, bacterial-endotoxin (LAL) testing for injectables, maintaining aseptic-technique competency, cleaning validation, and process validation. Cutting across all of them is the release bottleneck: the traditional fourteen-day sterility hold, which delays lot release, complicates planning, and is especially punishing for short beyond-use-date and precision-medicine products.

## Recent Industry Trends
- Heightened FDA scrutiny of sterile compounding following the 2012 fungal-meningitis outbreak.
- Persistent drug shortages driving demand for compounded alternatives.
- Growing adoption of automation, data-driven quality management, and rapid microbiological methods.
- Increasing overlap between 503B compounding and precision-medicine manufacturing, where short shelf lives make a two-week sterility hold untenable.

## Testing & Release Requirements
Typical release testing, varying by product type, includes sterility (USP <71>), bacterial endotoxin (USP <85>) for injectables, potency and assay by methods such as HPLC and UV-Vis, pH, particulate matter (USP <788>/<789>), and stability testing to establish beyond-use dating. Common analytical methods span HPLC, UV-Visible spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, and the Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) endotoxin test.

## Market Context

### Why 503B Compounding Matters
503B compounders fill critical gaps when commercial drugs are in shortage, serve patient needs that mass-produced medications cannot, and supply hospitals and clinics with specialised sterile preparations.

## Common Terminology
- **BUD** — Beyond-Use Date
- **ISO 5/7/8** — cleanroom cleanliness classifications
- **HEPA** — High-Efficiency Particulate Air (filter)
- **LAL** — Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (endotoxin test)
- **cGMP** — current Good Manufacturing Practice
- **CAPA** — Corrective and Preventive Action
- **OOS** — Out of Specification
- **SOP** — Standard Operating Procedure
- **Media Fill** — a process-validation technique using sterile growth media
